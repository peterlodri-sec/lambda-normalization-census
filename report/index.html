A machine-checked exploration of weak vs. strong normalization, the conservation phenomenon behind the Barendregt–Geuvers–Klop conjecture, and the exact size at which the untyped λ-calculus first separates them.

Abstract

What this is

The BGK conjecture asks whether every weakly normalizing pure type system is strongly normalizing — open since the 1990s. The positive sub-cases all rest on one phenomenon: in the erasure-free \lambda I-calculus, weak and strong normalization coincide (Church 1941). This page stress-tests that phenomenon by brute force: exhaustive reduction-graph analysis of hundreds of thousands of untyped λ-terms, executed in parallel on a 16-core box, plus an executable check of Klop's \lambda I-translation device. Everything here is computed, not asserted; the open conjecture is about PTSs and is not resolved by these untyped experiments — they corroborate the mechanism.

Experiment 1 · verification

Conservation at scale

Exhaustively enumerate every \lambda I-term (no erasing abstraction) up to a large size and classify each as WN and/or SN. The Conservation Theorem predicts zero WN-but-not-SN terms at every size.

Experiment 2 · research round

The λK separator census

Drop the \lambda I restriction. Now erasure is allowed, and WN-but-not-SN "separators" appear. Where does the first one show up, and how does their density grow?

Experiment 3 · benchmark

Reduction geometry of SN terms

For strongly-normalizing terms, the reduction graph is a finite DAG. We measure the longest reduction (maximal/perpetual strategy ≈ de Vrijer's L(\cdot)) against the shortest (normal-order), and the worst-case term-size blowup along the way.

Experiment 4 · verification

The perpetual-strategy law

A perpetual strategy is engineered to keep a reduction alive whenever possible. The theorem: it diverges iff the term is not SN. We test \text{(perpetual diverges)} \iff \neg\mathrm{SN} on every enumerated term.

Experiment 5 · verification

Klop's ι-translation, empirically

The device behind every WN⟹SN sub-case: translate an erasing λK-term into the extended \lambda I_{[,]}-calculus (parking discarded arguments in a memory pair [\,\cdot,\cdot\,]), where nothing can be erased. We implement the translation + its \pi-rule and check the transfer property \mathrm{SN}(M)\iff\mathrm{SN}(\iota(M)).

Experiment 6 · benchmark

Parallel speedup

The whole census is embarrassingly parallel. Fixed workload, cores ∈ {1,2,4,8,16}; we measure wall-clock speedup and efficiency against the ideal linear line.

Experiment 7 · visualization

Reduction-graph zoo

α-quotiented reduction graphs of famous combinators. A ¬SN graph contains a cycle (an infinite reduction); an SN graph is a finite DAG funnelling into normal forms (green).

Capstone · open research + data

The normalization census

The asymptotic version of the whole story: among all closed λ-terms of size n (standard de Bruijn natural size — our count sequence is exactly OEIS A275057), what fraction are SN, WN-but-not-SN (a separator), or non-WN? Exact for n≤16, Monte-Carlo (K=50k) beyond. The separator density and the smallest separator are, as far as the literature shows, unrecorded.

Bonus experiment · tooling

crabcc × the Linux kernel

A side quest: point the crabcc symbol indexer (the same engine that mapped this lab's own code) at the full Linux kernel source. Version 6.2 skipped C entirely (a tooling-only map); 6.3 parses C/Zig/YAML, turning it into a real kernel navigator. Indexed on a laptop in ~3 minutes.
